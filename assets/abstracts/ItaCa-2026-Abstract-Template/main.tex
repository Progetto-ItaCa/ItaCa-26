\begin{abstract}
For a small category $\mathcal C$, a presheaf $F$ on $\mathcal C$ assigns a set
to each object and a restriction map to each arrow. Which of these data can we
read off from a single functor? We take the category of elements of $F$ and
keep track of its projection $p\colon\!\int F\to\mathcal C$.

The fibre over $c$ recovers the set $F(c)$. To recover restriction along an
arrow $u\colon c\to d$, choose $y\in F(d)$ and lift $u$ to the unique arrow
with codomain $(d,y)$. Its domain tells us which element of $F(c)$ to choose.
\[
  \begin{tikzcd}[row sep=1.3em,column sep=3em]
    (c,F(u)(y)) \arrow[r] \arrow[d,mapsto] & (d,y) \arrow[d,mapsto] \\
    c \arrow[r,"u"'] & d
  \end{tikzcd}
\]
Uniqueness of lifts forces the identity and composition laws for the recovered
restriction maps. This gives the correspondence between presheaves on
$\mathcal C$ and discrete fibrations over $\mathcal C$. In the representable
case $F=\mathcal C(-,a)$, the category of elements is the slice
$\mathcal C/a$ and lifting amounts to precomposition. We use this example to
relate representability to the existence of a terminal object in the category
of elements~\cite{riehl}.

To see how the converse works, let $p\colon\mathcal E\to\mathcal C$ be a
discrete fibration and write $F_p(c)$ for the set of objects above $c$,
assuming these fibres are small. Given $y$ above $d$, define $F_p(u)(y)$ to
be the domain of the lift of $u$ ending at $y$. A composable pair of arrows
in the base has two apparent lifts with a prescribed codomain. We may lift
the composite directly, or lift the two arrows successively and compose.
Uniqueness identifies the two lifts, including their domains. This is exactly
the contravariant functoriality of $F_p$.

We can now compare the two total categories directly. An object $e$ of
$\mathcal E$ gives the pair $(p(e),e)$ in the category of elements of $F_p$.
For objects $e$ and $e'$, an arrow between the corresponding pairs consists
of an arrow $u\colon p(e)\to p(e')$ whose lift ending at $e'$ starts at $e$.
Thus forgetting down to $u$ and lifting back are inverse operations on arrows.
The comparison is an isomorphism over $\mathcal C$. A natural transformation
$F\to G$ likewise gives a functor between the categories of elements over
$\mathcal C$, by applying its component at $c$ to the chosen element over $c$.

Finally, the criterion for representability has a concrete interpretation.
An element $t\in F(a)$ gives, for each object $c$, a function
\[
   \mathcal C(c,a)\longrightarrow F(c),
   \qquad u\longmapsto F(u)(t).
\]
For a fixed $x\in F(c)$, its preimages are precisely the arrows from $(c,x)$
to $(a,t)$ in the category of elements. The functions are therefore bijective
for every $c$ exactly when $(a,t)$ is terminal. In the slice
$\mathcal C/a$, the terminal object is the identity of $a$. Under a chosen
representation it corresponds to the universal element $t$.

For a concrete example, take $\mathcal C$ to be a skeleton of the category of
finite sets and let $F$ send a finite set to its power set. An object of
$\int F$ is a pair $(X,S)$ with $S\subseteq X$. An arrow from $(X,S)$ to
$(Y,T)$ is a function $f\colon X\to Y$ satisfying $S=f^{-1}(T)$. What would
a terminal pair look like? For $2=\{0,1\}$ and $T=\{1\}$, every subset
$S\subseteq X$ determines exactly one such function $X\to 2$, its
characteristic function. Thus $(2,\{1\})$ is terminal. Reading off the
representing object from this pair recovers $2$, while its distinguished
subset records which of the two values means membership.
\end{abstract}
